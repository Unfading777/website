Thank you for showing me round, Sarah. You asked whether a kitchen-diner across the back of the house is possible and which route it needs. Here’s what I found from my visit and the public records for your home.

Best routePrior approvalA simpler application than full planning, for extensions over 3m and up to 6m deep.
Planning riskLowNo conservation area, listing or Article 4 found. Neighbours can still comment.
Decision time42 daysIf the council doesn’t decide in time, it’s approved by default.

What you told me

“The kitchen feels dark and cut off from the garden. We want one big space where the kids can play while we cook.”
  • Single-storey rear extension, about 4m deep and the full width of the house
  • Open-plan kitchen, dining and family space
  • Flat roof with a roof lantern over the dining table
  • Wide sliding or bi-fold doors to the garden

Three things to know first

  1. Up to 3m deep is permitted development, so no planning application, but I’d still get a Lawful Development Certificate to prove it. Your 4m design uses prior approval, where the council writes to your neighbours and has 42 days to decide.
  2. The wall on the attached side is on the boundary, so Party Wall notices will be needed. Serve them early.
  3. Building Regulations approval is needed whichever route you take. Wide glazing across the back will probably need an energy calculation at that stage.

Why I recommend a Lawful Development Certificate

  • It’s the council’s written confirmation that the extension is lawful, before you build.
  • Buyers’ solicitors often ask for proof when you sell. Without it, a sale can stall.
  • The rules are easy to get wrong, such as where the depth is measured from. The certificate settles it.
  • Building Regulations approval is still needed, whichever route you take.

Typical build costs in the South East

Published averages for 2026, to help you plan. They’re a guide, not a quote. Your builder’s price against the drawings is the real test.

Your kitchen, fees and a contingency are on top. A two-storey extension costs less per m² because it shares one set of foundations and one roof. A garage conversion is usually the cheapest way to add space.

What moves the price most

  • The width of the glazing to the garden
  • How deep the foundations need to go
  • Your choice of kitchen and finishes
  • The builder’s own workload and rates

Other things to budget for

  • Structural engineer
  • Party Wall surveyor, if a neighbour appoints one
  • Thames Water build-over agreement, if needed
  • A contingency: I suggest keeping 10–15% aside
